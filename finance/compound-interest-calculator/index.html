What is Compound Interest? A Simple Guide

Compound interest is one of the most powerful concepts in personal finance. It is the process of earning interest not just on your original money, but also on the interest you have already earned. In simple words — your money grows on top of itself, getting bigger and bigger over time.

💡 Simple example: You invest $1,000 at 10% interest per year. After year 1, you earn $100 interest — your total is $1,100. In year 2, you earn 10% on $1,100 (not just $1,000), so you earn $110. This continues to grow faster every year. That is compound interest.

Compound Interest vs Simple Interest — What is the Difference?

With simple interest, you earn interest only on your original amount every year. With compound interest, you earn interest on your original amount plus all the interest you have already earned. Over time, compound interest produces far more growth than simple interest.

How Does Compounding Frequency Affect Growth?

The more often interest is compounded, the more you earn. Here are the four main compounding frequencies used by banks and investment products:

  • Annually (yearly): Interest is added once per year. Most common for bonds and some savings accounts.
  • Quarterly: Interest is added 4 times per year. Common for many fixed deposits.
  • Monthly: Interest is added 12 times per year. Common for savings accounts and mortgages.
  • Daily: Interest is added 365 times per year. Common for high-yield savings accounts and some investments. Gives the highest return.

Why Regular Monthly Deposits Make a Huge Difference

The real power of compound interest shows when you combine it with regular monthly deposits. Even a small monthly contribution — like $100 or $200 — can dramatically increase your final balance over 10 to 20 years. This strategy is the foundation of retirement savings plans, mutual funds, and SIPs (Systematic Investment Plans) used worldwide.

Use the "monthly deposit" option in our compound interest calculator above to see exactly how much your regular savings can grow over time.

When is Compound Interest Used?

  • Savings accounts: Banks pay compound interest on your deposits
  • Fixed deposits (FD) and certificates of deposit (CD)
  • Investment funds and mutual funds: Returns are reinvested and compounded
  • Retirement accounts: 401k, IRA, EPF, PPF — all use compound growth
  • Loans and mortgages: Compound interest works against you when borrowing — lenders charge it on your outstanding balance
  • Credit cards: If you do not pay your full balance, interest compounds rapidly

The "Rule of 72" — How to Estimate Doubling Time

A simple trick to find out how long it takes to double your money: divide 72 by your annual interest rate. For example, at 8% interest, your money doubles in approximately 72 ÷ 8 = 9 years. At 12% interest, it doubles in just 6 years. This works best for yearly compounding and is used by investors worldwide as a quick mental calculation.

Tips to Make the Most of Compound Interest

  • Start as early as possible — time is the most important factor in compounding. Starting 10 years earlier can double or triple your final result.
  • Choose a higher compounding frequency — daily or monthly compounding gives better results than yearly.
  • Add regular deposits — even small monthly contributions grow significantly over time.
  • Never withdraw early — taking money out early breaks the compounding cycle and reduces growth significantly.
  • Reinvest your returns — in mutual funds, always choose the "growth" option so returns are reinvested instead of paid out.
Frequently Asked Questions
What is compound interest? +
Compound interest is interest that is calculated on both the principal amount and the interest that has already been earned. This means your money grows faster over time because you earn "interest on interest." It is the opposite of simple interest, which is only calculated on the original principal.
What is the compound interest formula? +
The formula is: A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n × t). Where A is the final amount, P is the principal (starting amount), r is the annual interest rate as a decimal (e.g. 8% = 0.08), n is the number of compounding periods per year, and t is the number of years. Our calculator does this math automatically for you.
How often should interest be compounded for the best results? +
Daily compounding gives the best results, followed by monthly, quarterly, and then yearly. However, the difference between daily and monthly compounding is usually very small. What matters much more is your interest rate and how many years you leave the money invested.
What is the difference between compound and simple interest? +
Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal every year. Compound interest is calculated on the principal plus all previously earned interest. Over long periods (10, 20, 30 years), compound interest produces far more growth than simple interest — sometimes several times more.
Can this calculator handle monthly deposits? +
Yes. Turn on the "Monthly Deposit" option in the calculator and enter how much you plan to add each month. The calculator will show you the final balance including all deposits plus the compound interest earned on those deposits over time.
Does compound interest work the same for loans? +
Yes, but it works against you. When you take a loan or use a credit card, the lender charges compound interest on the outstanding balance. This is why it is important to pay off debt quickly — the longer you take, the more interest you pay. For investments, compound interest helps you. For debt, it hurts you.
How accurate is this compound interest calculator? +
This calculator uses the standard compound interest formula used by financial institutions worldwide. Results are accurate for planning and comparison purposes. For exact figures from a specific bank or investment product, always check with your provider, as they may use slightly different calculation methods.
Which currencies does this calculator support? +
